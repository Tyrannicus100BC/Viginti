# Viginti — passing time in the ferry casino

Six first-person ambient-moment concepts. These extend the established five-card, three-horizontal-hand cabinet direction without reopening its layout. All images are generated staging studies rather than production 3D assets. No application code was changed.

## Tone and character

The player is a gruff robot traveler with ordinary creature comforts, questionable self-control, and a strong preference for being left alone. His hands can say most of that before he speaks: lingering over the Stand button during a call, trying to conceal a spill, or reaching for a drink before the server has finished offering it.

The casino is a long-haul ferry amenity. Its warm brass and drinks service promise luxury; the patched clothes, maintenance equipment, tired customers and indifferent hardware reveal something more ordinary. Staff have their own jobs and routines. Give the waitress competence and a sense of humor; avoid making her a flirtation reward or turning the player into a leering caricature. The space setting supplies occasional inconvenience as well as a spectacular view.

Not every moment needs a punchline, a plot reveal or a reward. Some should simply let the player inhabit this body and room. Avoid making the lounge secretly sinister by default; the comedy and quietness can support later tonal surprises without requiring them.

## 01 — The regular

Image: images/01-the-regular.png

The player looks right toward a cream-faced robot waitress in a burgundy vest and practical apron. The cabinet stays at the left edge as an orientation anchor. Her mechanical eyelids give her a tired, knowing expression; the player is already reaching for the drink.

Possible exchange:
- Waitress: “You said one more hand.”
- Player: “That was a different hand.”

Give her recurring variations rather than one endlessly repeated line. First she brings a drink; later she brings a coaster; after a spill she simply places several napkins beside him without comment. She can occasionally pass through the background without initiating an interaction. Familiarity makes this place feel real.

Camera/action: a seated head turn of roughly 35–45 degrees, brief eye contact, hand reaches to the tray, then back to the game. Offer a short acknowledgement rather than a branching conversation tree for every visit. The object handoff should make actual contact with the hand or shelf instead of disappearing between shots.

## 02 — Expensive trousers

Image: images/02-expensive-trousers.png

The player looks down at amber liquid on repaired work trousers and exposed knee plating. One hand wipes with an inadequate cocktail napkin; the other still has the glass tilted. This one frame suggests a second joke: he is concentrating so carefully on cleaning that he has not stopped making the mess.

Let the humor come from timing: a drip sound, a pause, a slow look down, a half-hearted wipe, then the realization that the glass is still leaking. A servo click or disgusted breath can replace a quip. No injury or dramatic electrical short is needed.

Camera/action: a downward glance from the same seat, not a new external view of the character. Legs appearing here are intentional, unlike persistent knees during normal play. Return to the cabinet with the stain still present for the rest of the session. The next waitress visit can pay it off with a larger stack of napkins.

## 03 — Still in transit

Image: images/03-still-in-transit.png

An orange wired receiver is held near the unseen ear at the edge of the frame; the left hand stays near the controls. The empty cradle and hanging cord show exactly where the phone belongs. In the background, a traveler with a suitcase and boarding signs remind us that the player is supposedly going somewhere.

Possible exchange:
- Caller: “Are you at the gate?”
- Player: “Near it.”

Calls can develop a small continuing thread: a dispatcher checking on a delivery, a traveling companion who went to find the gate, or an automated transfer reminder he keeps dismissing. Choose one thread for a run rather than making every call a new mystery. He can be evasive without the story needing to punish the player or terminate the game.

Camera/action: slight rightward lean and head tilt, receiver rises into the frame edge. Keep the cabinet as the main secondary object so his divided attention is legible. Do not put a phone UI over the card game. Cord and receiver stay on the same side of the cabinet across shots.

## 04 — Warranty void

Image: images/04-warranty-void.png

A short, cheap cigar, a small extractor fan, and an ashtray improvised with a bolt. The fan is the joke: it is technically present and trying, but does not quite solve anything. The player holds the cigar closer to it; the fan gets louder without becoming noticeably more useful.

Use this as a brief habitual pause, sometimes without dialogue. A gentle exhale, a tap of ash, the extractor rattle and the casino's room tone are enough. Another passenger can react occasionally, but avoid turning every puff into a repeated confrontation.

Camera/action: a modest left/upward look, raised hand with cigar at the edge of vision, return to the same rest position. Favor a small smoke ribbon; it should not obscure card choices or become a showcase of particle effects. A sleeping patron and other small background actions can continue while the camera visits this part of the room.

## 05 — The view is included

Image: images/05-the-view-is-included.png

A quiet glance away reveals the larger lounge: a gas giant and freight traffic outside, a sleeping robot at a neighboring machine, a janitor working around him, and a server crossing the room. The player's hand and a sliver of his cabinet keep the view connected to his seat.

The contrast is useful: an extraordinary view has become the ordinary background to waiting around. Do not force a joke or line here. Give the player a few seconds of altered room tone, low engine vibration, a distant announcement, or light passing across the floor as the freight tug moves.

Camera/action: optional look to the left, roughly 50–60 degrees, with a longer comfortable hold if the player wants it. The janitor's path, passenger and window remain in consistent places across visits. Small changes can reward looking again: the passenger is now asleep in a different pose, the janitor has moved, or a new ship enters the window.

## 06 — Gravity service

Image: images/06-gravity-service.png

For a moment, a drink, several amber droplets and a folded napkin lift off the side table. The player calmly catches the glass. A waiter in the background is already steadying the drinks on his tray. Everyone's practiced reaction sells how familiar this maintenance problem is.

Possible announcement: “Gravity service will resume shortly.”

The interruption should stay small. A few objects lift and settle; the whole room does not become a disaster. Cards on the cabinet are virtual and can remain stable while physical props move, making the cabinet's role within the world especially clear. A later visit can find a weighted coaster or a glass clip installed with very little explanation.

Camera/action: brief side-table glance, hand intercepts the glass, returning gravity brings the napkin down in a different position. Background waiter acts just before the player reacts, allowing repeat viewers to notice the anticipation. Use infrequently so it remains an event.

## How the moments belong to the game

Use a mixture of tiny gestures, optional conversations and quiet looks. A hand adjusting a cuff or tapping a cigar can take a second or two; a drink or phone beat might last several seconds; looking around can linger under player control. These are proposed pacing ranges, not fixed timers.

Keep the card state stable throughout. Schedule larger automatic head turns at quiet beats, such as after an action has resolved and before the next decision, and make returning to the cabinet immediate. Do not seize the camera during a card placement, challenge explanation, dealer reveal or scoring breakdown. A knock, ringing phone or approaching server can invite attention without forcing a head turn. Small peripheral actions can happen while the player continues to interact normally.

Once a prop is changed, keep that change: glass level, wet trouser patch, a moved napkin, ash in the tray, receiver in or out of its cradle. These modest traces connect the moments better than a stream of unrelated cutscenes. Use variations and long cooldowns; sometimes let the lounge remain quiet. No moment needs to cost cards, consume a turn, impose a timer or alter scoring unless a later explicit design calls for it.

The same body and room should be used in ordinary play and look-away moments. Do not swap in a separate cinematic hand model or rebuild the cabinet screen for each angle. The generated pictures illustrate staging and can contain local camera/prop inconsistencies; an actual 3D scene must fix the seat position, prop sides, reach distances and room map.

## Eventual rendering approach

Use a single seated body rig with forearms/hands animated in world space and a camera pivot at the head. Stage reaches to persistent prop sockets on the cabinet, tray and side table. Blend short hand/arm actions with optional head turns; the game state and cabinet display scene continue to exist throughout. Separate attention from consequences: camera motion does not itself advance a hand.

The props are deliberately few and reusable: one tumbler, napkin, receiver and cord, cigar/ashtray/fan, tray and waitress rig. Drink spills can use a small transient droplet effect plus a persistent stain decal or material mask; smoke can be a restrained particle ribbon; the gravity event can follow authored short motion curves rather than requiring a room-wide physics simulation. These are technique proposals, not implemented or performance-tested features.
